Jamaican law and practice directions

Binding in Jamaica

  • Legal Profession Act, ss 21, 22 and 35. Section 21 deals with written remuneration agreements, section 22 with the taxation of bills. Laws of Jamaica, Ministry of Justice.
  • Legal Profession (Canons of Professional Ethics) Rules, 1978, as amended. Canon IV(f) makes fairness and reasonableness the governing test for a fee, with time and labour one factor among several, alongside difficulty, required skill, customary fees, the amount involved, urgency, experience and reputation and whether the fee is fixed or contingent. See also Canon IV(a), (b), (g), (n) to (p) and Canon VII. The 2019 amendment rules added the obligations on sole practitioners to protect client property.
  • Legal Profession (Accounts and Records) Regulations, 1999, in the General Legal Council consolidation through 2017, with the 2019 amendment. Further amendments were gazetted on 4 June 2021 and 28 July 2021, effective 1 January 2022, and are published with the rest on the Council's legislation page. These govern client money and the accounting records a practice must keep.
  • Legal Profession (Contingency Fees) Regulations, 2018, effective 1 February 2019, regs 4 to 13. A signed written agreement is required and ceilings of 25%, 30% and 33⅓% apply depending on the stage reached.
  • Russell (Trudy-Ann) v General Legal Council [2024] JMCA Misc 2, Court of Appeal, 17 May 2024, at [33] and [46] to [72].
  • McGlashan (Winsome) v Nicholson Phillips [2022] JMCC Comm 36, Supreme Court, Commercial Division, claim SU2020CD00252, delivered 9 December 2022.
  • Supreme Court Practice Direction No 2 of 2018, assessment of costs, 31 January 2018. Published with the others on the Supreme Court's practice directions page.
  • Supreme Court Practice Direction No 1 of 2025, use of generative artificial intelligence in court proceedings, effective 17 September 2025.
  • Caribbean Court of Justice Practice Direction No 1 of 2025, 14 February 2025.
  • General Legal Council Annual Report 2022 to 2023, the source of the practising certificate figures used in the paper.

Citations follow the paper. Every link goes to the official publisher: the Ministry of Justice, the General Legal Council, the Court of Appeal, the Supreme Court or the Caribbean Court of Justice. Links were checked on 19 November 2026. Legislation is amended and pages move, so verify the current text and status of any provision before relying on it in a matter.

Comparative material, not Jamaican law

Comparative

  • Law Society of England and Wales, practice note on using artificial intelligence in legal practice, 21 October 2026. It is the Society's view of good practice for solicitors and is not legal advice. Its section on charging for AI-assisted work says:
    • The bill follows the agreement. What a firm bills should accurately reflect the charging basis agreed with the client.
    • Hourly work. Only time actually and properly spent may be recorded and billed. A firm should not bill for the time a task would have taken had AI not been used.
    • What may still be charged. Time spent operating the AI system, reviewing and verifying its output, revising the work and applying professional judgment, where that is consistent with the client care letter.
    • A separate technology charge. It should be clearly explained and agreed with the client in advance, and it should not duplicate an amount already recovered through hourly rates or another fee.
    • Fixed, capped or value-based fees. The scope of work and the charging basis should be explained clearly, and the overall fee must remain justified.
    • Telling the client. Firms are asked to consider being transparent in the client care letter about the extent to which AI is used.
    • An evolving area. The note prescribes no pricing model. It invites firms to consider how their billing arrangements should adapt and how AI-enabled efficiencies are reflected in the value delivered to the client.
  • Virginia Legal Ethics Opinion 1901, reasonable fees and the use of generative artificial intelligence, approved by the Supreme Court of Virginia on 24 November 2025 and effective from that date. It takes a different route from the Law Society and Queensland. In plain terms it says this:
    • The question it answers. How a lawyer may charge for work done with generative AI, mainly under fees that are not hourly, and how a fee is judged reasonable when AI makes the lawyer much faster.
    • No automatic cut. The opinion states that Rule 1.5 does not equate reduced time with proportionally reduced fees. A lawyer who finishes sooner with AI is not required to reduce the fee in step with the time saved.
    • Hourly work. An hourly fee can still rest only on the time the task took. The opinion gives no licence to bill hours that were not worked.
    • Fixed and other non-hourly fees. The fee may reflect efficiency, the lawyer's skill with the technology and the value of what the client receives. The same fee may be charged whether or not AI was used, provided it is reasonable on all the Rule 1.5(a) factors.
    • Time is one factor among several. Time spent and tools used should not decide the question alone. Skill counts, including knowing when to use AI, checking its output and catching its errors. So do the difficulty of the matter, the amount at stake, the result obtained and the lawyer's experience and ability.
    • Telling the client. A fee that rests mainly on skill and value has to be explained adequately to the client, all the more where AI has cut the time substantially. The opinion sets out no separate duty to disclose that AI was used.
    • What it leaves open. It does not deal with passing the cost of AI tools or subscriptions to the client. It accepts that market rates may fall over time, while holding that the rule should not force a lawyer to give up efficiency gains where the client still receives the value.
    Rule 1.5 is the Virginia rule on reasonable fees. The reasonableness of the fee remains the test throughout.
  • Queensland Law Society, Guidance Statement, Artificial Intelligence in Legal Practice, published 31 May 2024. Section 4.5 deals with legal costs and is the most practical of the three on billing:
    • Hourly work. Only time that can be measured and attributed to the matter may be charged. Entries must be accurate and may not be adjusted upwards to reflect how long the task would have taken by hand.
    • Who keeps the saving. Under time-based billing the benefit of time saved by AI belongs to the client, not the firm.
    • Fixed or hybrid fees. These may suit work in which AI plays a significant part. A fixed fee must still be fair and reasonable, and the firm may wish to keep records that justify it.
    • The cost of the tool. Adopting and supervising an AI tool, and per-user, lump-sum or annual licence fees, are practice overheads that cannot be billed to a client. A licence fee charged per matter may be a disbursement if the costs agreement says so.
    • Time on the matter. Time spent prompting the tool and checking its output for a particular client may be recorded and billed in the usual way.
    The Society now lists it as Guidance Statement No 37, though the published PDF bears the number 38.
  • Survey and market evidence from the United States, the United Kingdom and the wider Caribbean, discussed in the paper with its sample sizes and limits stated.

None of this binds a Jamaican practitioner, and the comparative material does not speak with one voice. England and Wales and Queensland approach AI efficiency through time actually spent, with Queensland adding that fixed or hybrid fees may suit AI-assisted work. Virginia approaches it through the value the client receives. The paper treats the debate as a foreshadowing of a conversation the profession here will have, and Canon IV(f) remains the Jamaican starting point either way.
